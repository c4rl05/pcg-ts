The 1889 tower, from Eiffel's plates

The eiffel demo builds the tower as it stood in 1889. Its large structure is Eiffel's own: every panel node where Plate XXXI of his 1900 album puts it. Everything below the panel scale — lattice struts, gusset plates, rivets — is placed by rule. This guide covers the sources, the rules, how the model was audited against the plates, and what it costs to cook.

Scope

Plates for the large structure, rules for everything smaller.

The tower's geometry divides at the panel. Above that scale the demo copies numbers: the node table of Plate XXXI, the floor levels of Plate I, the arch radii of Plates VII and XI, the member sections printed in Plate XXXI's tables. Below it, the demo states rules — which bracing pattern a panel carries, how a web member is laced, where a strut stops short of the upright it meets, where a rivet goes — and applies them to every member the table implies. Apart from a few members placed from their own figures — the summit's diagonal girders, the leg entretoises, one plan girder — no member is traced from a drawing.

The page offers three depths, and each is a different graph, not a different view of one. Skeleton draws every web member as a solid bar of its outer section. Laced replaces each bar with four corner angles and their lacing; in the legs and the shaft each is also trimmed at its joints and given gusset plates. Rivets adds the rivets as instances. The host page supplies materials, a sun, a ground plane and a unit rivet head; it generates no other geometry and corrects none, except to repair zero normals (see Rendering).

One camera pose at the three depths: panel 2 of a leg, on its lit face. The rivet heads in the third frame are 19 mm in radius and resolve only at full size.

Provenance

The primary source is Eiffel's 1900 plate album.

G. Eiffel, La Tour de trois cents mètres (Lemercier, 1900), is on Gallica as full-resolution scans, each plate in two halves of about 6000 × 8800 px. Every number read from a source is recorded in demos/eiffel/SOURCES.md with its source key and a tag: sourced (printed on a drawing or stated by a source), derived (computed from sourced numbers, arithmetic shown), estimated (read off a drawing by eye, or a printed digit that is ambiguous) or, from §10 on, measured (a pixel reading calibrated on a printed dimension). Where sources disagree, every value is listed. The constants the demo sets itself — the 50 mm joint clearance, the gusset reach, the 100 mm joint pitch, the 19 mm rivet head — are marked [estimated] in the code.

The conflict rules are applied the same way throughout. A plate beats a rounded official figure (57.63 m over the official 57 m). A plate at a larger scale beats one at a smaller scale. A printed number beats a pixel height, and a height is read on its plate's own vertical scale, never the horizontal one — Plate I is drawn at 15.27 px/m vertically against 16.14 px/m across, and a reading of Plate XI taken with its horizontal scale put the arch foot 0.5 m low. Where the album shows a post-1889 state, the 1889 state is taken from the text volume or from a figure titled for the 1889 Exposition. A later reading does not overwrite an earlier one: the later section of SOURCES.md names what it supersedes, so a value is quoted from the last section that gives it.

Secondary sources are cross-checks, not inputs. Rounded to two decimals, C. Chouard's 2001 transcription of Eiffel's documents agrees with the plate's full widths at 26 of 28 nodes; at IX (16.47 against the plate's 16.46244) and XXII (55.293 against the plate's 55.300) the two differ. A 2020 thesis that modelled the tower from the same drawings is cited where it is right and flagged where it is not: it reads the 36.00 m datum as 36 m of masonry, and gives panel 5 the slope of panels 1–4.

The large structure

Every upright is straight within a panel and turns only at a plate node.

Plate XXXI prints, for each panel, its height and its widths at its bottom node — the inner width and the leg's side below the second floor, all three from there to the merge at XI, one total above — from which the half-widths follow. The nodes are named by the plate's own Roman numerals, counting down from XXVIII at the bottom of panel 1 to I at the bottom of panel 28. spec.ts carries the table as 30 rows — the supports (panel 1's line extended to the support centres), XXVIII to I, and the top of panel 28 — and nothing between two rows is stored: a height between nodes is linear interpolation, exact because the uprights are straight. The curve of the tower's profile is a 29-sided polyline.

Heights use two datums. z is height above the ground at the tower's centre, the datum of the official floor heights; h is height above the support centres, the datum Plate XXXI is drawn on; z = h + 2.50. A half-width is the horizontal distance from the tower's axis to a member's axis, measured perpendicular to a face, as a face-on elevation shows it.

The table closes. Summing Plate XXXI's printed panel heights from the supports gives the top of panel 29 at h = 273.63038 m; the plate prints the third floor at 273.63 m. The residual is 0.38 mm over 273.6 m. The structural nodes XXIII and XVII sit 0.23 m below the first and second floors, which the chain also reproduces exactly.

The node table and the floor levels are byte-identical in the first commit and the last. spec.ts grew by 555 lines between them, most of it in the three audit rounds, and no node moved.

Architecture

TypeScript computes the tables. The graph holds only library nodes.

Six modules — legs, shaft, first floor, platforms, lifts, summit — build the tower. Each runs at graph-build time: it reads spec.ts, computes what its parts need in TypeScript — station positions, masks, face normals, end angles, outlines — and embeds the results in standard pcg-ts nodes as field parameters. The graph they produce uses 15 node types at the skeleton depth and 17 with rivets, none of them custom, so it serializes to JSON, and a test asserts that the skeleton graph's round trip cooks to the same positions.

A per-point table becomes a field through one helper, indexTable: ramp(index(), stops), exact at every integer because a ramp interpolates only between stops and no point reads a fractional index. Runs of equal values collapse to their two ends, which keeps long tables short; the largest has 5,946 stops. A ramp of more than 16 stops finds each point's segment by bisection, so a table with a stop per point costs n log n, not n². It used to scan its stops from the first, which is why the platforms module cooks its prism lists in runs of at most 1,024 points: splitting took its cook from 0.80 s to 0.31 s then. The runs are still in the graph and no longer needed: cooked as one list, with 194 fewer nodes, the skeleton depth is no slower (in an interleaved A/B of 35 fresh cooks a side, pinned as in the note below the table, it averaged 3% faster).

Each module returns meshes grouped by part class, and the graph declares one output per class: uprights, bracing, horizontals, girders, arches, platforms, masonry, summit, gussets (laced and rivets only) and names. At the rivets depth each rivet set is its own spawn and its own output: 41 of them, 859,816 instances in all.

Rules

Regions differ by which rules apply, not by how they are built.

Each region is a lattice of stations: a ring of positions repeated at every level, with station (s, k) stored as point k·R + s. A leg's ring is its four corner uprights, in the order c, b, a, d; the shaft above node XI has eight, the four corners and the four face centres. A web member is an edge rule: one connectByIndex node joining each station to the station ds along the ring and dk levels up, masked by a table that says where the rule applies.

Where one station grid describes a region, its parts differ only by which masks are set. In panels 1–4 each face carries two diagonals that run node to node and cross on a mid-panel horizontal; panels 6–9 carry one cross per face; in both, a laced central post runs through the crossing, parallel to the uprights. Panels 12–17 carry one X per panel. Panel 10 has two X's per face, either side of a post, over a band of flats. Panels 5 and 11 are lattice belts. Above node XVII the inner upright a has ended, and between adjacent legs a gap X braces b of one leg to d of the next in panels 12–16. Above XI the four legs are one shaft whose every face is two X-braced bays. From XVI up, every node except IX carries a horizontal truss across the tower, its pattern chosen by the node's name. Panel 10, the central posts and the gap X's have station grids of their own, and the belts, the Masque screen and panel 10's band are explicit member lists.

A swept chain puts one cross-section ring per vertex, in the bisecting plane. Where an upright changes slope at a node, the skin between two kinks would twist, and a seam of rivets along it would sit up to 11 mm off the steel. The upright lattice therefore adds a ring 0.01 m either side of every node where the slope changes, except XVII, where the upright is split instead; the kinked nodes are computed from the table, not listed.

Members

Every web member's section is squared to the face it leans in.

Every edge rule also writes the unit normal of the lattice face its member lies in, as a primitive attribute faceN: the cross product of the ring segment and the line joining that segment's midpoints on the two levels, exact for the trapezoid faces of a tapering section. A member's section axes follow from it — N = normalize(F − T(F·T)), B = N × T — so its section sits flat in its face. With the default +y up hint the section would be squared to the vertical instead, and in panel 1, where the faces lean 26.7°, that rolls one diagonal's section 30.4° off the face its rivets sit on and the other diagonal's 39.5°.

One detail makes this work in a graph: edge-rule members share their end points, and a sweep resolves its up hint per point, so a shared point cannot carry two faces' normals. Each member is first resampled onto two points of its own with pathResample, which carries the member's primitive faceN down onto them. The uprights are swept with a constant +x hint instead, which keeps a near-vertical member square to the tower's faces.

Members

A laced strut is four corner angles and two zigzags per face.

At the laced depth a web member's path becomes four chord paths, offset to the corners of its section, and eight lacing paths — two per face, in opposite phase, which puts one X in every lacing bay. The chords are swept as 90 mm squares and the lacing as 45 mm squares, both squared to faceN. The member types are the plates': the "800" web strut to 100.6 m, the "600" to 110.6 m, the "500" above.

In the legs and the shaft the lacing step is set per member, by the member's own outer width: 1.00 m for the 800, 0.94 m for the 600, 0.68 m for the 500. The first-floor and summit webs lace at 45°, and the entretoises at 0.79 m. Where the plates show a different step in a particular panel, a height band overrides it — the legs' 500s in panels 12–17 run at steps from 0.69 to 1.01 m, each read from Plate VII. The step is a field evaluated once per member on the primitive domain, from the member's mean height.

Members

A strut stops where its joint starts. The gusset plates cover the rest.

A web member run from upright axis to upright axis puts its lacing inside the upright's box — up to 15% of the lacing in the shaft, when the first version did exactly that. So each member end that meets an upright is trimmed to its joint start: the distance along the member at which its section clears the upright by 50 mm. A point a along the member and w off its axis lies a·sin t − w·cos t from the upright's axis, where t is the angle between member and column, which gives

jointStart = (r + 0.05 + w·cos t) / sin t

with r the upright's half-width at that height and w the member's in-plane half-width. The angle is recorded per member end by the edge rule, as the sine against the column's bisector at the station; at a kinked node the smallest of the sines against the bisector and the segments below and above is used, because at XXIII the bisector would place a horizontal's bottom chord 20 mm farther from the upright than it is.

Each end then receives a pair of gusset plates, one either side of the member's face plane, from the upright's face to jointStart + reach. The plates of the members meeting at a node overlap to form the node's gusset; no rule needs to know which members meet there, because each member places its own plates. While the joints were built (b0d0f01), an independent check found 496 joint rivets (1.7%) up to 127 mm inside an upright: the clearance had been a fixed distance along the member. Solved from the meeting angle, as above, it left none inside, with a minimum clearance of 173 mm.

Members

Three rules place the rivets, and each emits an instance batch.

Three rules place almost every rivet. A lacing rivet is where a lacing bar meets a chord: eight per strut section at every lacing sample, on the chords' outer faces. A seam rivet fastens a box member's corner angle to the plates it joins: on an upright, eight rows, 55 mm in from each corner, every 125 mm; the first floor's chords and posts carry the same rule. A joint rivet fastens a strut's two chords to each gusset plate, every 100 mm over the plate's reach and never nearer the upright than the joint start. No source prints a rivet pitch; 125 mm is measured on Plate IX's gusset detail, and the rules are not stretched to reach the 2.5 million rivets the tower is said to have. The summit adds three small rules of its own — bar ends, plate-girder rows, lattice nodes — for about 7,800 more.

Each rule ends in a spawnInstances node that emits a batch of transforms against the asset id "rivet". The batch carries the true size in its matrices, so the host resolves it against a unit head — a spherical cap of base radius 1 and rise 0.7, 80 triangles. Rivets receive shadows but do not cast them: a head 13 mm high throws nothing a 4096² map over the fitted 180 × 342 m frustum — 4 to 8 cm a texel — could resolve, and casting would put every instance into the shadow pass.

Before the first rivet commit landed, an independent check found rivets up to 19 cm inside a plate — a size table stepped 1 cm past a node, and the skin read that as an 11 m taper — and the commit fixed it. After the first audit round every rivet sat within 0.04 mm of its steel.

Library

One leg is built, and copied to four quadrants with its directions turned.

Every leg member is built once, in the +x+z quadrant, and copied onto four targets rotated 0, 90, 180 and 270° about the vertical. (The entretoises are the exception: they are built twice, as the Roux and Otis variants, each copied onto two legs.) copyToPoints composed each copy's position and rotation, but every other column rode along unturned: copied as-is, three of the four legs would carry a faceN pointing the source quadrant's way, and a box copied onto a turned target would be lit as if it had not turned. From its second commit the demo corrected faceN after the quadrant copy with a chain of three nodes and a quaternion written in the field grammar. That was recorded as a gap in the library, and fixed there.

copyToPoints now takes rotateAttrs: a list of f32 tuple-3 columns turned by each copy's target rotation, on the point, vertex and primitive domains. It is rotation only, so it is exact under uniform scale; it refuses P, rot, scale, seed and the bounds by name, with a message listing the valid columns. The default is an empty list and byte-identical to the old behaviour, so the corpus golden file did not move. Switching the leg members to it (12f0ec8) changed no output count; the turned normals agreed with the workaround to one f32 ulp and positions moved by at most 7.6 µm. Switching the box normals (09c2f9a) left 17.18 million position values bit-identical, with normals within 2.1 × 10⁻⁷.

Regions

The first floor, the lifts and the summit.

The first floor is built on one face in two planes — the face plane and a plane 15.00 m inboard, at the legs' inner uprights — and copied to the four faces. Its arch is two concentric circles, radii 36.4064 and 40.32373 m, turning to a lower arc of 44.5258 m where it meets the upright; 14 montants per half stand at a 4.011° pitch, and the arcade's heads are each the incircle of three lines, solved by Cramer's rule. The builder throws, naming the montant and the plate's rule, if the montants and the upright disagree.

The lifts follow the legs' curve because each rail station is anchored to the inner corner a at every Plate XXXI node, so a track kinks where its leg kinks: the Otis track holds 54.6° to node XXIV, steepens to 77° in panel 10, and runs at 74° to the second floor. The inclined cars are placed per leg, not copied, because each leg's car stands at a different height; the vertical lift on the axis runs two cars about the intermediate landing. The third audit round found tracks passing through solid decks — 90 intersecting triangle pairs — and the decks now have their openings from Plate XVI.

The summit was read wrong twice. Face-on, the structure above the third floor shows two 0.80 m columns at its corners, and two readings took them for a box. The third audit's blind reader established that they are two diagonal lattice girders, 14.870 m by 1.130 m, crossing over the axis — the north-south one continuous, the east-west one butting against its webs. Above them stand a dome of four half-arches, the lantern and the flagpole to 312.275 m.

The host page

The page draws what the graph settled, under a sun fitted to it.

The page cooks the graph once on the CPU, converts each class output to a buffer geometry and each rivet batch to instanced meshes, and gives each class one material in a single painted-iron family. A render that gave every class its own hue would read as a diagram; the classes are nudged apart only enough to tell them apart up close. The masonry is stone-coloured, the floors take a paint from the family's lighter end, and the names are the only gilded class.

A braced panel under flat light is a hatched silhouette. Lit from one side by a directional sun, it casts its web onto the members behind it and onto the ground, and the depth of the lattice becomes legible. The sun stands 49.2° up and is fixed. Its orthographic shadow camera is fitted to the union of the drawn geometry's bounds, with each corner's ground hit added so the thrown shadow falls inside the depth range. The shadow map is 4096², rendered once after the fit and again only when the visible classes change, so orbiting costs no shadow pass.

The one geometric intervention is a repair. meshPrimitive writes no normals, and mergePrimitives fills a column missing on one input with its default, (0, 0, 0), which a lit material shades black; several modules fell into this. They were fixed at source, the page replaces any remaining zero normal with its triangle's face normal, and the library trap is in the backlog. The depth buffer is logarithmic, because the page shows rivet heads from half a metre and the whole tower from 1.5 km.

Method

One agent session wrote the rules. Other agents checked them against the plates.

The demo was built in one Claude Code session, from the first request at 18:09 on 2026-09-18 to the last commit at 14:17 the next day, in 18 commits. The main thread launched 45 agents, and those launched their own readers and verifiers: 107 agent transcripts in all. The choice at the start was between tracing members and stating rules; rules were chosen, with the plates as the first authority for anything a rule takes as input and an [estimated] tag where no source gives a number.

01ResearchOne agent compiled the first sections of SOURCES.md (§0–§9) from the plates: it fetched the Gallica IIIF manifest, identified the plates from contact sheets, downloaded the full-resolution views, and read printed figures off cropped, magnified regions. Two web agents gathered the official figures and the profile mathematics.
02Arithmetic checkA separate agent re-derived every [derived] value of the first spec in its own code, told not to read the research agent's script: 254 sub-checks at 2 mm, 0.02° and 0.5% tolerances, all passing.
03Build, verifiedBuild commits were re-derived by agents that were not given the answer; five commit messages record such a check (e906699, 0aca149, 1a806f0, 1a40d99, 12f0ec8). The first found web members squared to the vertical, 30° off in panel 1, fixed in the next commit; later ones found rivets up to 19 cm inside plates and girder plaques cutting 21 mm into the uprights, each fixed in the same commit.
04AuditFour auditors — legs and shaft, first floor, summit, whole silhouette — each wrote down the expected value with its tag, measured the plate where the spec was silent or estimated, cooked the model and measured it, and tabulated ratio and verdict. They fixed nothing.
05FixOne fixer per module, each required to confirm every finding on the plate before changing a number, and to keep the better-supported value where it disagreed with the audit. Fixes were written up in SOURCES.md, most with a was/now table.
06Re-audit, blindRound two used fresh auditors that had not seen the fixes, told to trust neither the fixers' claims nor the code comments, and plate readers with no access to the model, the code, the spec or any report. Round three's auditors re-derived each expected value themselves with blind helpers, and listed the fixers' claims they could not reproduce.

Every pixel reading is calibrated on a dimension printed on the same figure. The silhouette audit deskewed the Plate I scan by 0.87°, rendered the model orthographically face-on at 4096² (9.30909 px/m), took each row's full ink extent as the median over ±0.4 m, and counted a row as off only if it was off by more than 3% under two calibrations — one isotropic, one mapped piecewise through the printed levels. Member sections were measured on the cooked meshes, sliced perpendicular to each member.

Limits

What the model does not claim.

The large structure is the plates' to the precision the plates print. Below the panel scale the model is a set of rules whose parameters are plate readings — often [estimated] or [measured] — so most members are where their rule puts them, not where the drawing shows that particular member. The rivet pitch is a measurement of one detail applied everywhere, and the model carries 859,816 rivets, not the 2.5 million the tower is recorded as having. The names are plaques without lettering. The 1900 album occasionally draws a post-1889 state, and where the text or an 1889 figure does not settle it, SOURCES.md says so.

The build contains no randomness. The graph's seed, 1889, reaches only per-point seed columns that nothing reads; the same source cooks to the same bytes on every run, which the demo's commits use as their control when a refactor claims to change nothing.
